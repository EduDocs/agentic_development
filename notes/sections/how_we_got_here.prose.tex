\chapter{From Words to Agents}
\label{chapter:HowWeGotHere}
% EVOLVE-BLOCK-START

The agentic AI systems this course builds rest on large language models (LLMs), and those models can seem to have arrived all at once.
They did not.
The capability that makes an agent possible (a single model that reads instructions, holds a conversation, writes code, and calls tools) is the accumulated payoff of a collection of significant advances, each of which removed a concrete limitation of the one before it.
This chapter traces that lineage at a high level, highlighting a few milestones.
The goal is not to teach any one method but to give the reader a timeline they can hold in their head: enough of the story to see that today's models are a consequence rather than an accident, and to talk with some fluency about how the field arrived here.

A single question runs through the whole progression, asked in progressively more powerful ways: how should a machine represent and manipulate language?
Each answer below made the next one reachable.
The dates are close together; the span from the first contributions outlined below to working agents is little more than a decade.
This is part of why the current moment feels sudden even though the path is legible in hindsight.

\section{Meaning as Geometry (2013--2014)}
\label{section:MeaningAsGeometry}

An important step in natural language processing (NLP) was to give words a form a machine could compute with.
The obstacle is that text arrives as discrete symbols, and a symbol supports no arithmetic: nothing in the character strings \emph{physician} and \emph{doctor} reveals that they are close in meaning, while \emph{physician} and \emph{physicist} look nearly identical and are not.
A \emph{word embedding}\index{Word embedding} maps each word to a vector (an ordered list of numbers, typically a few hundred, corresponding to a point in a high-dimensional space). 
Because text has no natural numerical scale, these coordinates are learned rather than hand-designed: the training objective asks the model to predict a target word from its surrounding context, pushing words that share similar environments toward nearby coordinates in this high-dimensional space.
The \emph{word2vec}\index{word2vec} models showed that such a space could be learned cheaply from raw text at scale (Mikolov et al., 2013).
A companion result added an efficient training objective and vectors for phrases, and displayed the now-famous regularity that simple vector arithmetic is meaningful in this space: subtracting the vectors for \emph{Germany} and \emph{Berlin} yields their relationship, and then adding that to the vector for \emph{France} will land near \emph{Paris} (Mikolov et al., 2013).
Meaning had become geometry: a trained space in which distance encodes similarity.

\begin{example}
Think of a scatter plot generated from Principal Component Analysis (PCA)—a standard technique that reduces high-dimensional data tables down to a few principal components, often visualised on a two-dimensional map.
In such a plot, data points cluster based on shared statistical traits, and spatial proximity reflects overall similarity.
Word embeddings work the exact same way, with one distinction: the coordinate axes are learned automatically from raw text rather than calculated from tabular measurements.

Each word becomes a single point in this space.
Terms like \emph{ecosystem}, \emph{biodiversity}, and \emph{habitat} form one tight cluster, while terms like \emph{inflation}, \emph{unemployment}, and \emph{monetary} form another.
The two neighborhoods sit far apart simply because the two fields rarely mix their vocabularies.
No human labeled those groupings, and no linguist manually defined those relationships.
The clusters emerge entirely from patterns of word co-occurrence across billions of sentences.
\end{example}

This is the direct ancestor of the embeddings the course uses for retrieval (Chapter~\ref{chapter:Retrieval}, Definition~\ref{definition:Embedding}), one representational level down, words rather than passages.

Representing isolated words, however, is not the same as handling an entire sentence.
The next milestone was mapping an input sequence to an output sequence.
\emph{Sequence-to-sequence}\index{Sequence-to-sequence} learning achieved this using two linked networks: an \emph{encoder} that compresses an input sequence into a single vector, and a \emph{decoder} that reads that vector to generate the output sequence (Sutskever et al., 2014).
These models were \emph{recurrent}: they read text one \emph{token} at a time (a token being a word or sub-word chunk) while updating an internal memory, much like processing sequential or time-series data step-by-step.
The system was trained \emph{end-to-end}, meaning both networks were optimized jointly against final translation accuracy without relying on hand-built grammar rules or manual dictionary alignments.

The weakness of this setup was precisely that single summary vector: forcing an entire input sentence through one numerical bottleneck lost detail on long inputs.
The arithmetic makes the pressure concrete: a four-word title and a forty-word paragraph are both squeezed into the exact same budget—say, a fixed list of 1,000 numbers.
The longer the input, the more information is squeezed out before the decoder ever sees it.
Removing that bottleneck was the next breakthrough.


\section{The Transformer and the Age of Scale (2017--2018)}
\label{section:TransformerEra}

\emph{Attention}\index{Attention} arose as a solution to this bottleneck: rather than forcing the model to rely on a single summary vector, attention allows a network to dynamic compute a weighted score for every input word, selectively focusing on the most relevant parts of the input sequence at each step. 
Additive attention first let a recurrent decoder look back across all intermediate encoder states rather than a single fixed vector (Bahdanau et al., 2015), relieving pressure on long inputs while retaining sequential recurrence.

The mechanism acts as a dynamic weighted lookup, and stating it plainly is essential since virtually all modern AI architectures build upon it. 
When generating a specific output token, the model evaluates every input position, scores its relevance, and converts those scores into weights that sum to one (much like a weighted average in statistics). 
It then retrieves a weighted sum of the inputs based on those scores. 
Instead of compressing information upfront, the model dynamically selects relevant context as needed, using attention weights learned during training.

The \emph{Transformer}\index{Transformer} took the more radical step of discarding sequential recurrence entirely and building the architecture out of attention alone (Vaswani et al., 2017; Figure~\ref{figure:Transformer}). 
By allowing every position in a sequence to attend directly to every other position simultaneously, it made training over massive datasets practical through parallel computing. 
Recurrence requires step $t$ to finish before step $t+1$ can begin; attention evaluates all token interactions at once using matrix operations, efficiently utilizing thousands of parallel processors. 
Every large language model used in this course is a Transformer.

This mechanism carries a hard computational cost: calculating pairwise attention between every token means compute and memory scale \emph{quadratically} with sequence length ($O(N^2)$). 
Doubling the input length doubles the comparison work per token and quadruples total compute—the difference between one million pairwise comparisons for 1,000 tokens and ten billion for 100,000 tokens. 
It also introduces a behavioral challenge: in extremely long contexts, a model's effective focus per token can dilute. 
This empirical phenomenon—often called a finite ``attention budget''—is explored alongside its limits in Chapter~\ref{chapter:ContextEngineering}.

The key takeaway is that the architecture relies entirely on attention and feed-forward layers, removing sequential recurrence entirely.

The Transformer was an architecture; the next move was a \emph{recipe} for deploying it.
Rather than training a bespoke model from scratch for every specific task, researchers realized they could \emph{pre-train}\index{Pre-training} a single, massive Transformer on raw, unlabeled text and then adapt (or \emph{fine-tune}) it to specific tasks with minimal additional training.
The underlying economics shifted completely.
Human-annotated data is expensive, domain-specific, and scarce, whereas raw text is abundant.
The expensive, general-purpose step of learning how language works happens once; that computational investment is then amortized across every downstream task.
A laboratory that previously had to train a custom classifier on a few thousand hand-labeled scientific abstracts could now start with a model that had already read billions of web pages and adjust it using those same abstracts—achieving higher accuracy at a fraction of the cost.

\emph{BERT}\index{BERT} pioneered this approach using a \emph{bidirectional} encoder, meaning the model evaluates words by looking simultaneously at the context both to the left and to the right (Devlin et al., 2019).
Its pre-training task was a scale fill-in-the-blank exercise: hide a word, predict it from its surrounding context, and repeat billions of times without human supervision.
This produced \emph{contextual} embeddings, where a word's vector dynamically updates based on its sentence: in \emph{river bank} and \emph{central bank}, the word \emph{bank} receives two distinct vectors, whereas static word2vec models assigned one vector that averaged both meanings together.
Contextual embeddings from this encoder family now power modern document search and retrieval systems.

A parallel effort pre-trained \emph{decoder} models optimized for generation rather than classification: the \emph{Generative Pre-trained Transformer} (GPT) series.
The two branches split by objective:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encoders (e.g., BERT):} Read a whole passage at once to evaluate, classify, or match text.
    \item \textbf{Decoders (e.g., GPT):} Predict the next token given previous words, emitting text sequentially—the core mechanics needed when the goal is to generate natural language.
\end{itemize}
This decoder branch is the direct lineage leading to the promptable models and agents used in this course.


\section{Scale, Prompting, and Grounding (2020)}
\label{section:ScaleAndGrounding}

Scaling the decoder branch produced a qualitative change, not merely a quantitative one.
\emph{GPT-3} was a 175-billion-parameter model that, with no task-specific training, could perform a new task given a plain-language instruction and a handful of examples placed directly in its input text: \emph{in-context}, or \emph{few-shot}, learning\index{In-context learning} (Brown et al., 2020).
Three terms in that sentence carry the weight:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parameters:} The model's learned internal weights fixed at the end of training. At 175 billion parameters, GPT-3 represented a roughly hundredfold scale increase over its predecessor (GPT-2).
    \item \textbf{Context Window:} The maximum length of text (measured in tokens) the model can process and hold in view at a single moment.
    \item \textbf{Few-Shot Prompting:} Demonstrations of the desired task live directly inside the input text (the \emph{prompt}) at runtime, rather than being learned into the model's parameters via iterative mathematical adjustments (\emph{gradient updates}).
\end{itemize}
This marked the birth of prompting as a primary software interface: instead of retraining model weights for every new problem, the model is steered entirely by the text it is given—the foundational premise of this course (Chapter~\ref{chapter:ContextEngineering}).

\begin{example}
Suppose an economist wants journal abstracts categorized into \emph{empirical}, \emph{theoretical}, or \emph{both}.
The standard route available before 2020 was to hand-label a few thousand abstracts, train a dedicated classifier, and repeat the entire engineering exercise for the next classification scheme.
The in-context route is to write out what the three categories mean, paste five already-sorted abstracts as demonstrations into the prompt, append the target abstract, and read the answer.
Not a single weight changes, and nothing is trained; the complete specification of the task is a plain-language paragraph that a domain expert can evaluate, refine, and revise in seconds.
That property, more than any accuracy figure, is why prompting became the primary interface: it moved the point of control from the machine-learning pipeline to the researcher's own description of the problem.
\end{example}

It is also where the honest limits come into view: few-shot performance is uneven, and training on web-scale text introduces risks of data leakage and benchmark contamination (Chapter~\ref{chapter:Evaluation}).
\emph{Contamination} names a specific hazard: when the training corpus is scraped broadly from the open web, the test questions of a standard evaluation benchmark may already reside inside the training dataset.
In such cases, a high score can reflect memorization of the answer key rather than the underlying reasoning capability the benchmark was designed to test.

Furthermore, a \emph{frozen} model—one whose internal weights are fixed and no longer updating—has knowledge locked at its training cutoff date: it cannot cite specific source locations, and its internal facts are difficult to update.
\emph{Retrieval-augmented generation} (RAG)\index{Retrieval-augmented generation} addressed this by pairing the model's \emph{parametric memory} with an external \emph{non-parametric memory}: retrieving relevant passages from a document database, then generating an answer conditioned directly on those passages (Lewis et al., 2020).

The two memory systems differ fundamentally:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parametric Memory:} The internal weights absorbed across trillions of tokens during training. It is fast and flexible, but unattributable—no single fact lives at a distinct, editable memory address.
    \item \textbf{Non-Parametric Memory:} An external, searchable document repository (such as a database of PDFs, spreadsheets, or text files). A new paper can be added on Tuesday and consulted on Wednesday without retraining or altering a single model weight.
\end{itemize}

Concretely, a model whose training ended last year cannot know a newly published trial protocol or economic index.
A RAG system searches the research group's document store, inserts the three most relevant passages directly into the prompt context, and answers using those sources.
This architecture makes AI knowledge continuously updatable and, when the system preserves document provenance (source attribution and citation tracking), fully auditable—forming the core foundation of grounding developed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:Retrieval}.


\section{From Models to Agents (2022 onward)}
\label{section:FromModelsToAgents}

A capable, promptable model is still not an agent.
Further technical advances were required to close that gap.

First, \emph{instruction tuning}—often fine-tuned using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)—aligned models to respond to requests helpfully rather than simply predicting plausible text continuations (Ouyang et al., 2022).
That distinction is much sharper than it sounds.
A raw language model trained solely to predict the next word, handed the prompt \emph{"List three limitations of this assay,"} might simply generate a fourth and fifth exam question without answering any of them.
In a massive text corpus scraped from the web, the most likely follow-up to an exam question is another exam question.
Instruction tuning transforms the model from a passive text predictor into a responsive assistant that executes commands.

Second, \emph{chain-of-thought} prompting demonstrated that asking a model to generate step-by-step intermediate reasoning before emitting a final answer dramatically improves performance on multi-step problems (Wei et al., 2022).
When asked for a final complex value outright, the model must commit in a single generative step.
When prompted to write out intermediate calculations first, it conditions each subsequent step on its previous reasoning—yielding significant gains on multi-stage tasks such as chained unit conversions, economic equilibrium derivations, or conditional-probability calculations.

Finally, equipping models with \emph{tools}—permitting them to issue external commands, inspect the output, and adjust their next action—transformed text generators into active systems.
This iterative process forms the core reason-and-act loop developed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:AgentLoop} (Yao et al., 2023).
By requesting external execution (such as running a Python script or querying a database) and reading the returned data, the model ceases to merely converse: it perceives environmental feedback, evaluates results, and acts iteratively.

\begin{example}
A \emph{tool} is nothing more exotic than an external function the surrounding hosting program is willing to execute on the model's behalf: run a Python script, read a file, query an SQL database, or fetch a web page.
The language model itself executes no code.
Instead, it emits a \emph{structured request} (such as a standardized JSON block naming a function and its arguments); the host application executes that function, captures the output, and feeds the result back into the prompt context as plain text.
The model then reads that output and determines its next step.

Under this setup, a question such as \emph{"Does the regression fit improve if the outliers are dropped?"} is no longer answered from stagnant memory.
The model generates the code to fit the model, requests its execution, inspects the returned residual summary, notices that a predictor variable has become unstable, and automatically revises the statistical model.
That iterative loop represents the entire functional distance between a system that merely describes an analysis and one that actively carries it out.
\end{example}


The techniques for improving how a model reasons and acts constitute a major research field; naming this landscape helps anchor concepts developed in later chapters.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prompting Strategies:} 
    Standard chain-of-thought prompting has evolved into search-based extensions.
    \emph{Self-consistency} samples multiple reasoning paths in parallel and selects the majority answer, while \emph{tree-of-thoughts} evaluates and branches over intermediate steps rather than committing to a single path.
    Frameworks like \emph{ReAct} (interleaving \textbf{Re}asoning and \textbf{Act}ing) and \emph{Reflexion} (incorporating self-reflection and memory to debug errors) allow a model to evaluate tool execution outputs and refine its approach dynamically (Chapters~\ref{chapter:ContextEngineering} and~\ref{chapter:AgentLoop}).

    \item \textbf{Training Approaches:} 
    Beyond prompt engineering, models are fine-tuned directly for better alignment and reasoning.
    Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is the foundational method here. Its relatives include RLAIF (learning from AI feedback) and \emph{Constitutional AI}—a framework that replaces manual human grading of harmful outputs by using an explicit, written set of principles (a ``constitution'') to direct a secondary AI model to critique, revise, and score responses automatically.
\end{itemize}

The core intuition behind RLHF and RLAIF is worth keeping in mind: evaluators rank competing model responses to the same prompt, those rankings train a separate \emph{reward model} that scores responses, and the language model is optimized against that score.
This allows complex preferences that cannot be written down as a formal mathematical loss function (e.g. clarity, safety, or tone) to be learned directly from human rankings.

Overall, making a model reason and act effectively is an active, evolving field rather than a single trick; this course builds directly on these core paradigms while providing the context needed to place new advances.

The progression did not stop at tool use.
Recent advances fold step-by-step reasoning directly into the architecture itself: \emph{reasoning models}\index{Reasoning model} are trained using reinforcement learning to perform extended internal deliberation before emitting a final answer, rather than relying on users to prompt them to think step by step.
Concurrently, models have become increasingly \emph{multimodal}, processing visual inputs alongside ordinary text.

These native reasoning capabilities carry important practical consequences, particularly regarding transparency and control (developed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:AgentLoop}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internal (Latent) Reasoning Traces:} The model's raw internal deliberation process is optimized strictly to maximize problem-solving accuracy, not for human readability. Consequently, this hidden reasoning trace cannot be treated as a faithful, verifiable audit log explaining \emph{why} a model arrived at a specific conclusion.
    \item \textbf{Thinking Budgets vs. Prompt Scaffolds:} External prompt structures designed to force multi-step reasoning (such as complex chain-of-thought templates) are replaced by a direct \emph{thinking budget}—a configurable control that allocates computational compute dynamically to allow longer internal deliberation before responding.
\end{itemize}

These developments shift the developer's role: rather than manually constructing complex prompting scaffolds, the objective becomes setting deliberation budgets and directing systems that reason natively.


Each step so far has been described from the inside, as one more technical limitation removed.
From outside the research community, the same sequence registered as two events.
In November~2022, the release of ChatGPT put an instruction-tuned model in front of anyone with a browser and sent a shock wave well beyond the research community, showcasing the language model as a general-purpose advisor.
Three years later, in December~2025, agentic coding crossed a practical threshold: on narrow, well-specified programming tasks whose success can be checked automatically, a frontier model such as Anthropic's Opus~4.6 approached the reliability of a junior software engineer or data scientist, turning much routine low-level coding into something a researcher directs rather than writes by hand, freeing attention for the parts of the work that call for judgment.
The qualifier carries weight: reliability falls off as tasks widen, as the specification thins, or as success stops being cheaply verifiable, so the threshold is best read as one crossed within a regime rather than across programming as a whole.
\emph{Cheaply verifiable} is the load-bearing phrase, and it means only this: an automated check settles the matter without a person reading the work (a test suite that passes, a simulation that reproduces a known result, a residual that drops), so an agent can iterate against it unattended, whereas without such a check someone must inspect every attempt.
Neither was a new idea so much as a new level of a capability the sequence above had been building toward.
The progression is therefore still under way, and its shape is unchanged: each step extends what the system can reliably do on its own.
Still, a legible, incremental progression is often felt from outside as a sudden arrival.

\section{The Progression Was Not Random}
\label{section:NotRandom}

Read as a list, the milestones above are a handful of loosely related papers.
Read as a chain, each one removed a specific limitation of the last.
This is a \emph{selected} teaching lineage, not a claim that each advance was the sole cause of the next; larger datasets, faster hardware, and reinforcement learning from human feedback run alongside it.
Word vectors gave meaning a computable form but handled only isolated words; sequence-to-sequence mapped whole sentences but squeezed them through one vector; the Transformer removed that bottleneck and made scale practical; pre-training made a single model's knowledge transferable across tasks; scale made that model steerable by plain text; retrieval grounded it in external, updatable knowledge; and instruction tuning and tool use let it act.

None of these steps was guaranteed in advance, and their nearness in time is why the field's current state is genuinely recent.
But the sequence is not arbitrary: today's agents are where this particular chain of problems and solutions has led, and the same pressure that drove it --- extend what the system can reliably do --- is what drives the rest of these notes.

For a researcher, this history is not a spectator's story.
On tasks that are well-specified and verifiable (writing and debugging analysis code, sweeping a method across many datasets, drafting a derivation, screening a design space), a capable agent can compress work that once took hours or days into minutes: an order-of-magnitude acceleration of the research loop, sometimes two, enough on some tasks to change what a small group can investigate independently.
The gains are uneven and should not be overstated; they shrink where judgment, genuine novelty, or hard-won trust is the bottleneck rather than throughput.
Where they do apply, though, the acceleration of the research loop is real.
It is not confined to computer science; a biologist fitting population models, an economist searching model specifications, a physicist proposing and testing functional forms, and a chemist screening candidates all face the same well-specified, checkable inner loops.

The reason to attend to this moment is therefore not novelty but \emph{discovery}: the parts of research that reduce to propose, run, check, and repeat are exactly the parts an agent can turn, and turning them faster changes the pace of finding things out.
Learning to direct that process and, by the capstone, to let agents search on their own is what these notes are for.
